Teaching

Teaching Philosophy

My teaching philosophy is centered on helping students move from learning individual technical skills to confidently applying those skills in complete engineering systems. My goal is for students to understand not only how to perform a task, but why it is done, how it connects to the larger system, and how they would troubleshoot or adapt that process in a professional environment. I want students to leave my courses with both technical knowledge and the confidence to work independently with unfamiliar equipment, documentation, and engineering problems.

A major focus of my recent instructional development has been creating a stronger connection between lecture, simulation, laboratory work, and real-world application. In my robotics courses, I have been restructuring the learning sequence so that students first encounter a concept in a practical setting, develop a deeper understanding of the underlying theory and engineering principles, and then apply that knowledge on physical industrial equipment. This approach allows students to immediately see the relevance of topics such as coordinate systems, tool frames, programming, registers, I/O, PLC communication, and system commissioning. I am also placing greater emphasis on the use of technical documentation, troubleshooting, design iteration, and individual competency so that students learn how to find and apply information rather than relying solely on instructor-provided procedures.

My broader goal is to continue developing courses that increasingly resemble the way engineering work is performed in industry. Students should have opportunities to design components, program and commission equipment, diagnose failures, document their work, and integrate multiple technologies into functioning systems. I am particularly interested in creating project-based experiences where students progress from structured introductory activities toward greater independence and system-level responsibility. Ultimately, I want my teaching to produce students who are curious, technically capable, willing to experiment, and prepared to approach complex engineering systems with a disciplined process of research, testing, troubleshooting, and continuous improvement.

When I began teaching Industrial Robotics at Utah Tech, I helped develop a new curriculum and restructured the course to teach more of the principles behind robot motion, including static and inertial forces, along with I/O and Ethernet communication. I was also the first instructor to bring FANUC RoboGuide into the course, and I helped develop a PLC trainer so students can practice group I/O, digital logic, and PLC-to-robot Ethernet communication on real hardware (see Educational Projects).

Goal

Enhance the industrial robotics curriculum with robot I/O and PLC integration to provide students with more comprehensive robot training and improve their employability in industrial automation.

Approach

Develop hands-on lab activities that progress from robot I/O and discrete wiring to Ethernet communication and PLC-coordinated robot operation.

Goal

Expand the Industrial Robotics curriculum to give students a stronger understanding of complete robotic systems, including setup, tooling, operation, and troubleshooting.

Approach

Develop new lectures and hands-on labs using FANUC technical resources that emphasize end-of-arm tooling, robot setup, fault recovery, and system-level troubleshooting.

Goal

Integrate FANUC RoboGuide into the robotics curriculum so students gain experience with offline programming and virtual system design while developing skills used in industrial robot integration.

Approach

Develop RoboGuide labs that let students build, program, test, and troubleshoot virtual workcells before using the physical robots. This gives students more opportunities to practice and iterate, makes physical lab time more productive, and allows more advanced robotic applications to be explored without requiring additional hardware.

Goal

Successfully teach MTRN 2200 and MTRN 2205 for the first time while building a strong understanding of the curriculum and ensuring students develop safe, practical industrial wiring and troubleshooting skills.

Approach

Follow and evaluate the existing course structure, equipment, labs, and assessments while identifying where students struggle. Use those observations to improve lectures, demonstrations, lab instructions, and assessments for future semesters.

Process Optimization & Surrogate Modeling

Dispense weight depends on motor frequency (Hz) and run time, but the relationship is nonlinear and noisy in practice. To find repeatable settings for any target weight, I collected paired (time, Hz, weight) trial data and trained two surrogate models to predict dispensed weight from process settings: a degree-2 polynomial regression (RMSE 0.346 lbs) and a small neural network, 4→16→16→1 (RMSE 0.303 lbs). I then inverted the fitted relationship into a closed-form estimate, Time (ms) ≈ 10,140 × lbs0.894, and used the models' surrogate surfaces to search for the frequency and time pair that hits each target weight with minimum predicted error.

All four optimized settings (Accel/Decel = 1.0s) landed within 0.05 lbs of their target weight.
